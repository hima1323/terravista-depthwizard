2Abstract

DepthWizard turns one optical satellite image into a height map of every building, tree and slope, and lets disaster managers fly through it in 3D and check it against reference data.

It is team TerraVista’s (IIT Ropar) answer to Smart India Hackathon 2026 problem SIH26175, “Single-View Height Estimation and 3D Flythrough”, set by ISRO under the Disaster Management theme. The height model is RS3DAda (Song et al., NeurIPS 2024), used with its released weights and no fine-tuning by us.

For GeoTIFF inputs, DepthWizard turns those heights into an absolute surface model in metres above sea level. It uses the Copernicus GLO-30 terrain model with roofs and trees removed, NASA ICESat-2 laser ground points and optional ground control points. A local, offline app adds a three.js flythrough, in-app validation (MAE, RMSE, correlation) and four disaster tools: Prepare, Respond, Relief and Assess.

Tested zero-shot, the model’s height error is 3.90 m [3.24, 4.66] on 150 aerial tiles against airborne LiDAR, where a flat-ground guess scores 5.65 m, and 4.85 m on eight Indian scenes against ICESat-2. It reproduces the published satellite results for Atlanta and Buenos Aires. Its main weakness is that objects taller than 20 m come out 15–18 m too low. One full GeoTIFF scene runs end to end in 42 s on an 8 GB laptop.

3Definition of the Problem

3.1 Problem statement

Estimate a reliable height for every pixel of a single optical satellite image, in metres, and show how far those heights can be trusted.

Disaster managers need the height of buildings, trees and ground within hours of an event, but most of India has no recent 3D survey. Optical satellite images exist for almost every place and date, yet one image is flat: it shows what is there, not how tall it is.

3.2 Origin and identification of the problem

The problem comes from ISRO, which set it as SIH26175 in Smart India Hackathon 2026. Behind it is a practical gap. India’s disaster managers lack up-to-date, high-resolution height data. Airborne LiDAR is costly and rare. Stereo satellite pairs, which give height directly, are not always available for the place and date of an event. Free 30 m terrain models miss individual buildings. The statement therefore asks for:

  • a relative surface model (DSM) from a PNG or JPG image, and an absolute DSM in metres above sea level from a GeoTIFF;
  • scale calibration from a low-resolution terrain model (DEM) or a few ground control points (GCPs);
  • a 3D flythrough in which users check heights against reference data using RMSE, MAE and correlation;
  • results that hold across urban, sparse, hilly and forested land;
  • a standalone, offline tool with documentation.

3.3 Methodology adopted to identify the problem

The problem reached us as a defined statement, so our work went into finding where the real gap lies. We used four methods:

  1. Study of the problem statement. We split SIH26175 into 13 testable requirements and traced each one to a feature (README requirement table).
  2. Literature review of monocular height estimation, global terrain models (Copernicus GLO-30, FABDEM, SRTM, CartoDEM) and laser missions (ICESat-2, GEDI).
  3. Discussions within the team and with mentors, to decide what a disaster manager needs from a height map.
  4. Benchmarking existing DEMs against NASA laser data. Before building anything, we measured how far the free terrain models sit from ICESat-2 ground points in Indian towns, hills and forests.

We did not run surveys, key-informant interviews or focus-group discussions. [TEAM TO FILL: who we spoke to and what we learned]

3.4 Detailed description of the identified problem

Three gaps make single-image height hard to use in practice.

  • Height from one image is ambiguous. From above, a tall building and a short one can look alike. Models learn cues such as shadows and roof size, and they under-estimate tall objects. On our tests, objects above 20 m come out 15–18 m too low.
  • Height above ground is not height above sea level. Flood and drainage analysis needs absolute elevation. Free terrain models are 30 m per pixel, and in towns and forests they include roofs and canopy, so their “ground” is too high. In our two urban Indian test scenes, raw GLO-30 sits 5.28 m from laser-measured ground on average.
  • No one can tell how wrong the map is. Most tools give a height map with no error figure. A relief officer cannot tell whether a 2 m flood depth is good to half a metre or to five.

The tool must also work across India’s very different landscapes, from dense cities to hill towns and river banks (Figure 1).

3.5 Current developments in the domain

  • Monocular depth models. Vision transformers with dense-prediction heads (DPT) now estimate depth from a single image. Remote-sensing versions predict height above ground instead of camera distance.
  • RS3DAda and SynRS3D. Song et al. (NeurIPS 2024) trained RS3DAda on SynRS3D, a large synthetic dataset of rendered cities, and adapted it to real images without labels (Figure 2). It is the height model we use.
  • DINOv2. Meta’s self-supervised image encoder gives general features that transfer well to aerial and satellite images. RS3DAda uses its ViT-L/14 version.
  • Global terrain models. Copernicus GLO-30 is free worldwide at 30 m. FABDEM removes buildings and forest from it, but its licence is non-commercial.
  • Satellite lasers. NASA’s ICESat-2 (ATL03 photons, ATL08 ground and canopy classes) and GEDI give accurate heights along tracks across India.
  • Indian sources. Cartosat imagery and CartoDEM from NRSC exist, but access is login-gated, so they are not yet tested here.

3.6 Need and significance of resolving the problem

A trustworthy height map from one image would let disaster teams:

  • estimate which streets and homes a given water rise would reach, before the flood;
  • route rescue teams away from deep water and steep ground;
  • pick safe helipad and relief-camp sites;
  • count affected buildings after the event.

Today these tasks rely on coarse 30 m terrain models or are not done at all. Closing that gap, and saying how accurate the result is, makes the output usable for decisions.

We chose RS3DAda for three reasons. It was trained for remote sensing, so it predicts height above ground rather than distance from a camera. It predicts land cover in the same pass, which the terrain step needs to find roofs and trees. And its weights are public, so anyone can reproduce our numbers. We did not fine-tune it: every test in this report uses the same released weights.

6Detailed Work Plan and Technological Interventions

A scene passes through seven steps, from one uploaded image to a checked surface model in 3D.

  1. Input. The user picks a scene or uploads a PNG, JPG or GeoTIFF (Figure 5).
  2. Preprocess. The image is cut into overlapping tiles so scenes of any size fit the model.
  3. AI model. RS3DAda predicts each tile four times (original and flipped) and averages the results.
  4. Height. The tiles are blended into one height-above-ground map with a land-cover map.
  5. Calibrate. For a GeoTIFF, the app fetches GLO-30, removes roofs and trees, and levels the terrain to ICESat-2 ground photons when two or more laser passes cross the scene. Ground control points, if given, refine the result.
  6. Verify. The user compares heights with a reference raster, surveyed points or ICESat-2, and gets MAE, RMSE, bias and r by landscape.
  7. DSM + 3D. The absolute DSM is shown as terrain you can fly through, and exported as GeoTIFF or GLB.

How the terrain is cleaned and levelled

Step 5 is where most of the engineering went. GLO-30 is a surface model, so in towns and forests its “ground” includes roofs and canopy. For each 30 m cell we subtract the model’s mean height above ground, and trust the cell in proportion to how much of it the land-cover map calls open ground. Cells covered by roofs or trees are filled from their neighbours.

The cleaned terrain is then compared with ICESat-2 photons that NASA classes as ground, and shifted by the median difference. With only one laser pass the app refuses, because a single line can carry a local DEM error into the whole scene. The N5 rule also drops “ground” photons that sit on a building and more than 3 m above the other ground photons.

7Novelty and Innovation of the Proposed Interventions

The height model is published work; what is new is how DepthWizard turns its output into numbers people can trust and act on.

  • Laser-levelled absolute heights from one image. Most single-image tools stop at relative height. We strip roofs and canopy from GLO-30 using the model’s own output, then level the result to ICESat-2 ground photons. In our two urban Indian scenes this terrain is 2.36 m from laser ground, against 5.28 m for raw GLO-30 and 2.80 m for FABDEM.
  • Safety rules learned from failures. The app refuses to level from a single laser pass. In Silent Valley one pass along a forested valley floor would have shifted the whole scene by 46.59 m. The N5 roof filter stops ICESat-2 photons labelled “ground” on rooftops from being used as ground.
  • Honest uncertainty. Building blocks carry a 90% interval with measured coverage. The per-pixel flip spread is labelled a “consistency score”, not an error bar, because it ranks errors on flat ground (Spearman 0.44) but not on tall objects (−0.03).
  • Validation inside the tool. Users upload a reference raster or surveyed points, or fetch ICESat-2, and get the ISRO metrics by landscape. For ICESat-2, the passes used for levelling are never used for checking.
  • A claims register. Every number in this report, the README and the deck links to the result file it came from, with a 95% confidence interval and a baseline at the same points.

8Approaches to Implement the Intervention Plans

DepthWizard can reach users in four ways, from a single laptop to national platforms.

  • Standalone offline app for field teams. A district or state office installs it with one click and runs it offline. Internet is needed only to fetch terrain, laser points and rain forecasts for a new scene. One full GeoTIFF scene took 42 s on an 8 GB Apple M3 laptop.
  • Integration with NDMA / SDMA GIS workflows. The outputs are standard GeoTIFF and GLB files, so they load into QGIS, ArcGIS, Bhuvan or a state GIS without the app.
  • Cartosat input. India’s own imagery and CartoDEM would replace the commercial demo basemaps. A data request to NRSC is drafted but not yet sent.
  • A distilled small model for laptops (planned). A smaller student model, trained to copy RS3DAda and exported to ONNX for fast CPU use, is planned. It is not yet part of the app, and its accuracy will be reported only once measured.

Each rollout would follow the same steps:

  1. Validate on Cartosat imagery and CartoDEM for a few Indian districts.
  2. Pilot with one disaster-management office during a monsoon season, comparing flood-reach maps with observed extents.
  3. Train staff with the user guide and a short hands-on session.
  4. Collect feedback and errors through the in-app validation results.

The reference data has limits of its own. In dense towns, NASA’s ground class can include rooftops: in Chandigarh every one of the 35 held-out “ground” photons was a roof. The N5 filter now removes such photons, so Chandigarh has no laser check until a pass with real ground is available. Most laser passes are also years away from the image date, and India has only two test scenes per landscape.

Design issues

  • Offline first. Heavy data (terrain, laser photons) is cached per scene, so a scene opened once works without internet.
  • Honest labels. Disaster outputs are labelled scenarios, not forecasts, and the flip spread is not presented as an error bar.
  • Speed against accuracy. The full ViT-L model is the most accurate but slow on CPU. A faster model must be measured on the same tests before it can replace it.

11Expected Outcomes

The main outcome is an offline tool that gives a checked height map from one image. It beats a naive baseline on every height test and matches the published model on satellite imagery.

Three results stand out. On 1,450 GAMUS buildings the error is 3.57 m, against 10.54 m for a flat-ground guess. On eight Indian scenes the model alone is 4.85 m from ICESat-2 segment heights, against 8.25 m for “height 0”. Laser levelling removes the terrain’s average offset (bias +2.58 m to −0.27 m) but not its typical error.

The results also show where the tool can be trusted. On GAMUS it is most accurate in urban areas (2.90 m, against 5.66 m for flat ground) and least accurate under trees (6.67 m). On sparse, open ground a flat-ground guess is better (0.55 m against our 0.96 m), because predicting zero is already right there. The absolute DSM adds detail rather than average accuracy. Against GEDI canopy and roof tops it beats the raw GLO-30 DSM (5.97 vs 8.39 m), but against all ICESat-2 photons it does not (9.14 vs 8.06 m).

Flood scenario test, Porto Alegre, May 2024

To test the flood tool, we flooded the pre-flood image of four small windows and compared the result with the observed extent. With ICESat-2 levelling the agreement (CSI) rises from 0.20 with raw GLO-30 to 0.79. This is a scenario test, not a forecast: the windows are small, and one would not be levelled by the current single-pass rule.

Beyond these numbers, the expected deliverables are:

  • relative and absolute DSM GeoTIFFs for any uploaded scene;
  • a 3D flythrough with video export for briefings (cover image);
  • per-scene validation reports in the ISRO metrics;
  • flood-reach, route and relief-site maps, labelled as scenarios;
  • open documentation and a claims register, so others can reproduce each number.

12Suggested Plan of Action for Utilisation of the Expected Outcome

The outcomes should reach users in four stages, each gated on evidence from the one before.

  1. Close the accuracy gaps.
    • Reduce the tall-object bias, for example with shadow length and sun elevation, a correction per land-cover class, or a canopy-height model for trees, without making low objects worse.
    • Validate on Cartosat imagery and CartoDEM, with more Indian scenes per landscape.
    • Test a drainage-aware flood model (HAND) on one real Indian flood (Assam, Wayanad 2024 or Himachal 2023) against a Sentinel-1 or NRSC flood extent.
    • Measure the planned small student model on the same benchmarks before offering it as a fast mode.
  2. Pilot with one State Disaster Management Authority for one monsoon season: pre-monsoon flood-reach maps for a few flood-prone districts, compared with observed flood extents.
  3. Hand over to a nodal agency. Share the code, documentation and claims register with NRSC / ISRO, so the model can run on their GPU servers and feed Bhuvan.
  4. Teach and extend. Use the tool in geography and planning courses, and invite students to add Indian test scenes.

Beyond disaster management, the same height maps can support urban planning, rooftop-solar studies and checks on unauthorised construction.

13Conclusion

One optical satellite image, combined with free terrain and laser data, can give a usable and checkable height map offline.

DepthWizard beats a flat-ground guess on every height test, reproduces the published model’s satellite results, and cuts town terrain error against laser ground from 5.28 m to 2.36 m. It runs a full scene in 42 s on an ordinary laptop.

It is not yet a replacement for a survey. Tall buildings come out 15–18 m too low, forest terrain is about 20 m off, and India’s own Cartosat imagery is still untested. Because each result comes with its error and a baseline, users can see where to trust it and where not to. The next steps are fixing the tall-object bias, validating on Indian data, and piloting with one disaster-management office.
